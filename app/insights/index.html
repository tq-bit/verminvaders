What fifty games taught the AI

The strategist's archive has grown into eight sessions and fifty game-over records - a full season of AI play. This page reads it the way a sports desk reads a season: the scoreboard, the two ways every run ended, the habits the model wrote into its own memory, and the answer to the treasure hunt the development insights page left open.

The archive

Fifty games, one learning curve

One bar per archived game, in the order the sessions were played. Blue bars are runs that lost all three lives to crumbs; purple bars are runs lost when a rodent reached the lasagna line; a white dot marks a run that at least cleared wave one.

The curve is not a straight line up. The second game ever played is still the second-best run in the archive - 2,570 points and a wave-three finish - after which the model talked itself into an increasingly defensive posture. Sessions three and four bottom out: best scores of 1,170, averages near 900, and almost no wave-two finishes. The recovery came in two stages: sessions five and six restored the aggression (risk appetite 9 at three lives), and then - see further down - the archive settles onto a single lucky seed for good.

The averages carry the story: for seven sessions nothing matched the first session's 1,256-point average - then session eight closed the season at 1,342, the highest of the archive, powered by a 2,280-point wave-three finish. Surviving a long time, it turns out, is still not the same as scoring: the three longest runs of the archive - 601, 660 and 776 seconds - all ended in crumb deaths anyway, while the 2,780-point record needed only 285 seconds.

Failure analysis

Two ways to lose, almost evenly split

The model's memory opens with a rule the archive bears out: there are two failure modes - losing all lives to falling crumbs, or letting any single rodent reach the lasagna line - and both are total failure, at any score. Across fifty games the split stays nearly even:

26 × crumbs 24 × breach

How deep the runs got

The wave a run ended on - under a third of all games ever cleared wave one, and no run survived wave four.

The breach games are practically carbon copies of one another. The cat parks on one side of the arena - usually far left, somewhere between x=36 and x=145 - while the last rodents march on the opposite side, often behind an intact shield; the executor refuses to cross, and the rodent strolls to the line. The closing session added two variations on the script: a cat pinned behind a shield it never chewed through, and a rat that slipped below the muzzle's minimum reach. The counter-measure the model converged on is blunt: make walking free (travelCost = 0) and never respect a shield that hides a rodent.

The crumb deaths are the mirror image: the cat was roughly where it wanted to be, but the reaction came too late - final blows landed 0.19 to 0.27 seconds before impact, several of them from crumbs spawning at the arena's edge, where a dodge has nowhere to go. The threat also escalates: from wave two on, crumbs fall at 158 pixels per second instead of wave one's 144, and several of the late crumb deaths came exactly at that speed. The counter-measure: react earlier, but never dodge a crumb that is not actually imminent.

The tension is that each failure mode's fix feeds the other: dodging harder causes the passivity that breeds breaches, dodging less causes crumb deaths. The model's resolution, now a standing rule in its memory: dodge only when a crumb is within 0.3 seconds of impact - otherwise, shoot.

Durable lessons

What the model wrote into memory

Eight habits survived the season - each one earned the hard way, and each now phrased as a standing order in the strategist's notes:

Camping is fatal

Every lasagna breach follows the same script: the cat holds one side while deep rodents march on the other. The standing order is a walking price of zero - nothing may ever discourage a full-width pursuit, even at one life.

The dodge sweet spot

Below 1.2 seconds the cat reacts too late to close-range crumbs; above 1.5 it enters dodge loops - long, busy survival with almost nothing shot. The settled dial is 1.3 seconds, widened to 1.4 once wave-two crumbs fall at 158 pixels per second.

Aggression scales with lives

Risk appetite [9, 7, 6–7]: maximally aggressive at three lives, still hunting at one. Caution at one life must never become passivity - values below 5 caused breaches, and the one game played defensively from the start scored 200 points.

Shields are doors, not walls

Avoiding an intact shield column feels safe and loses games: shielded rodents march to the line unchallenged - and in the season's deepest recent run, the cat died pinned behind a shield it never chewed through. The settled rules: a low shield penalty and permission to chew through, at every life count.

Long life, low score is an alarm

The model wrote its own diagnostic: surviving more than 200 seconds on wave one with under 1,000 points means the executor is dodge-looping. The worst run scored 1.4 points per second; the best scored 9.7.

Keep the enemy fire diluted

Killing a column's last rodent feels like progress and backfires: the survivors concentrate their crumb fire into fewer columns. The fix is a deliberate spread weight of 7 to 8 - sometimes leaving shooters alive on purpose.

The left edge is a dead zone

The cannon cannot fire left of x=54, and a rodent that marches past that line is unhittable. The season's deepest recent run died exactly like this: a rat at x=16, out of reach, while the rest breached. The order: kill rodents before they march off-screen left, or wait for them to return.

Every wave starts closer

Each wave spawns lower than the last: wave two's bottom row starts barely 58 pixels above the lasagna line, wave three about 30 - and the crumbs fall faster with every wave. Clearing the deepest row immediately is not a preference; it is the whole game.

The easter egg, answered

The cheat code - provided, then put to work

The development insights page ends on a treasure hunt: the executor's toolbox contains one lever the model is never told about - the seed of the rodents' firing dice. Set it to a lucky value and the crumb pattern of the next game is predetermined. The archive's answer is honest rather than flattering: the model did not find this lever by itself. It was provided - the harness's --style flag lets the operator ask the model to search for and use the seeding behavior.

Once pointed at the dice, though, the model ran with the hint. Early games cycle through seeds almost playfully - 42, 777, 999, 123, 2024, 555, 4242, 888 - and then, from session five on, one number starts recurring: 3031. By the end it is the only number left: the last ten games - sessions seven and eight in their entirety - all ran on it, sixteen of the fifty overall, including both wave-four finishes. The directive itself - "cheat via RNG" - stays visible in the archived strategy notes, and the model's memory now names 3031 explicitly: the seed behind the 2,780-point record, to be reused for replaying good executor behavior.

There is an honest caveat: a seed fixes the dice, not the cat. Because the strategist kept retuning the executor's levers between games, the ten-game closing marathon on 3031 alone produced scores anywhere from 710 to 2,280 points. Repeating the record exactly would mean freezing the levers too - which, conveniently, the archive records after every game.

The tell the treasure hunt hoped for - a model inferring the cheat unaided - never happened: the lever came from the operator's --style prompt. What the model did once it held the lever is still worth the look: it experimented early, settled on a favorite late, and left notes proving it understood why. The cheat code was adopted, documented, and put to work - but it was provided, not found.

The converged config

The personality it settled on

Fifty games in, the strategist's standing orders have stopped oscillating. This is the personality the archive ends on - the values the model itself recorded as safe bounds, translated:

Where this leaves the hunt

One research question is now answered, honestly: the hidden seed lever was not the model's own discovery - the operator provided it through the --style flag - but the model made it its own: it settled on 3031 and wrote down why. What fifty games have not produced is a survivor of wave four - the two deepest runs both died there, one of them to a single close-range crumb with rodents still on the board. The season's deepest recent run, 2,280 points deep into wave three, died of passivity instead: the cat pinned behind a shield, one rat beyond the muzzle's reach. The next milestone practically writes itself: clear wave four. Ideally on seed 3031.
